In-Class Peer Review

In-class peer review is where you read your teammates' rough drafts and give them feedback on their writing — and where you get feedback on yours. It happens during class, on the day marked on the calendar.

At a Glance

  • You will review two of your teammates' rough drafts.
  • For each paper, you'll do two things:
    1. Part A Paper Map — a quick reverse outline that helps you (and the author) see the paper's structure.
    2. Part B Feedback on the Draft — comments on specific spots where things are working well or could be stronger.
  • All of it happens in Microsoft Word online, on the copy in your team's shared OneDrive folder.
  • Bring a laptop or tablet.

If you can't make peer review: in case of emergency or illness, see the alternative assignment at the bottom of this page.

Where Peer Review Happens

Peer review is done during class in Microsoft Word online, through your Macomb Microsoft 365 / OneDrive account.

  • Before class, everyone uploads their rough draft to their team's shared OneDrive folder. The link to that folder is posted in Canvas, on the Rough Draft assignment.
  • During class, I'll help everyone make sure the groups are even based on attendance, and you'll give two of your teammates feedback directly on their papers in Word Online.
  • Bring a laptop or tablet so you can comment on your teammates' drafts and read the comments left on yours.
  • You will need to sign in with your Macomb account in order to access this folder.

Overview: The Two Parts

Your job is to read your teammates' papers as a reader — not as an editor. You're helping the author see their paper from the outside: what's coming through clearly, what's confusing, where the analysis feels convincing, and where it feels thin.

For each paper you review, you'll complete two parts:

  • Part A Paper Map — Read the paper all the way through and build a quick outline of what you see. Type this at the end of the document, under a heading with your name.
  • Part B Feedback on the Draft — Go back through and leave comments in the margins, pointing to specific spots that are working well or could use improvement.

Do them in that order. Part A gets you the lay of the land, so the comments you leave in Part B are about the paper as a whole rather than the first thing you happened to notice.

You may discuss papers out loud with your team, but make sure your feedback is also left in writing on the draft — that written record is what your teammate takes into revision, and what shows me the review was done.

Part A Paper Map

Start by reading the paper all the way through and building a Paper Map. This is a quick reverse outline that helps you get the lay of the land before you start leaving detailed feedback. It also gives the author a useful picture of how their paper reads to someone seeing it for the first time.

Paper Map Questions

Type your Paper Map at the end of the document, under a heading like Paper Map — [your name] (not as margin comments). Answer the following:

  1. What community and what genre?
    In a sentence: what discourse community is this paper about, and what genre from that community is being analyzed?
  2. What conventions does the paper analyze?
    List them. Each one might have its own paragraph or section, or they might be mixed together — just describe what you see.
    If you're having trouble telling the conventions apart, that's worth noting. It usually means the organization could be clearer.
  3. What genre samples show up for each convention?
    For each convention on your list, do a quick tally: how many different genre samples are used as evidence? A rough count is fine.
  4. Does the support feel sufficient?
    Is any convention resting on a single example? A convention is supposed to be a pattern in the genre, so one example usually isn't enough to show it. Are there sections that feel thin, or places where a claim about the genre isn't backed by anything from the samples?

Part B Feedback on the Draft

After finishing the Paper Map, go back through the paper and leave comments in the margins in Word online, on your teammate's draft in the shared OneDrive folder.

What to Look For

Focus on high or middle order concerns (see section below for more on this). You do not have to answer all of the following questions, but you can use these if you're unsure what to comment on:

  • Does the introduction give enough background for someone who has never heard of this community to follow the rest of the paper?
  • Are the genre samples actually analyzed, or are they described and then left alone without explaining what they show? (i.e. do the convention paragraphs include TED — topic sentence, evidence, and discussion — or are they missing that last part?)
  • Is each convention shown as a pattern across more than one sample, or does it rely on a single post or example?
  • At some point, does the paper connect the genre back to how the community works — what it values, who belongs, how people talk to each other?
  • Are there spots where you got confused or had to re-read to understand what was being said?
  • Is the paper easy to follow? Does the organization make sense?

Feedback Requirements

  • Leave at least three comments that:
    • address high- or middle-order concerns that could use improvement (organization, details, paragraph structure, examples, flow, transitions, evidence use, etc.).
    • point to a specific spot in the paper that illustrates the issue.
  • Leave at least one comment about something that's working well (ideally connected to a high-/middle-order concern).
  • Do not leave later-order concerns (grammar/spelling/mechanics) unless they block readability. If so, make one note to explain the issue.
  • Do not edit your classmate's draft (no direct grammar fixing in the text).

High- and Middle-Order Concerns

Both parts focus on these. Not grammar.

High-Order Concerns: The Big Stuff

Think about assignment requirements, overall organization, audience, purpose, and genre.

  • Does the paper meet the assignment prompts and use evidence from genre samples?
  • Is it written as an academic analysis essay for an appropriate audience?
  • Do ideas appear in a logical order, and does the paper stay on topic?

Leave feedback on any area that could be improved.

Middle-Order Concerns: The Effective Writing Stuff

  • Are paragraphs well-developed (e.g., Topic-Evidence-Discussion)? Are claims supported with quotes, descriptions, or screenshots from genre samples?
  • Are there transitions connecting ideas? Does the draft feel choppy or disconnected?
  • Any sentences that are too long, awkward, or hard to read?

Leave feedback on any area that could be improved.

Tips for Giving Good Feedback

Keep in Mind

  • You don't have to prescribe exact fixes. Focus on describing what you notice as a reader. "This section felt like it jumped topics" is more helpful than trying to rewrite it for them.
  • It's fine to comment as a reader. For example, "I'm getting a little lost in this spot" or "I'm not sure what you mean here" is fine.
  • Use feedback sandwiches when possible (from the "In-Class Peer Review" reading). Pair suggestions for improvement with something that's working well nearby.
  • Be specific. "The third paragraph is confusing" is okay. "The third paragraph starts describing the posts but never says what they show about the genre" is much better.
  • It's a rough draft. Expect it to be rough. Your job is to help the author figure out where to focus their revision energy, not to find everything that needs fixing.
  • Later-order concerns: no grammar or spelling feedback. Exception: for a consistent issue (e.g., fragments/run-ons) that hurts readability or flow, you can mark it once to flag the trend.

Viewing Your Feedback

Open your own draft from your team's shared OneDrive folder. Your teammates' comments appear in the margin alongside your paper, and their Paper Maps are at the end of the document. They stay there, so you can go back to them while you work on your Revised Draft.

Alternative to In-Class Peer Review

If illness or an emergency prevents you from attending peer review, you can complete an alternative assignment: getting feedback on your draft from a writing tutor at the Academic Success Center.

Book early — and don't come to class sick

The alternative requires an appointment with a writing tutor at the Academic Success Center, and the ASC has limited availability. Appointments fill up.

This alternative is for illness and emergencies. As soon as it looks like you won't be able to make peer review, book the appointment. Putting it off usually means there is nothing left to book, and "I couldn't get an appointment" is not something I can fix after the fact.

And please — don't show up to peer review sick. That is exactly what this alternative is for.

It has to be a face-to-face appointment

To count as peer review, the session must be an in-person or Zoom appointment with a tutor — a conversation where you and the tutor work through your draft together.

  • Asynchronous written comments will not count. The point of peer review is the back-and-forth, so the alternative has to be a live one.
  • Make the appointment through the Penji link in Canvas (the light blue penguin icon), or the Penji website.

Come to work, not to be corrected

Plan to actively work with the tutor one-on-one to improve your paper. This is a working session, not a proofreading drop-off. Focus the appointment on high- and middle-order concerns — organization, flow, use of evidence, paragraph development, whether your analysis is coming through. See High- and Middle-Order Concerns above for what those are.

Bring your draft, your genre samples, and the assignment page. Come with something you already know you're unsure about — tutors can help far more when you can point at the part that isn't working.

Use the same feedback focus as in-class peer review

Your session needs to cover the same things your teammates would have given you feedback on — see Part B: Feedback on the Draft and High- and Middle-Order Concerns above. Take notes during the session on the feedback you receive; you'll need them for your revision.
